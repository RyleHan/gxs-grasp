\begin{abstract}
Language-driven grasp detectors feed the instruction to the whole network, although the sentence only
decides \emph{where} to grasp, not how wide or at which angle. We factorise grasp quality into a
language-free \emph{graspability} map $G$ and an instruction-conditioned \emph{selection} map $S$.
$G$ is a GR-ConvNet trained on the union of all grasps annotated in an image; $S$ compares frozen dense
CLIP features with the instruction and only has to choose among graspable regions. Both are supervised
by labels Grasp-Anything++ already has: every image carries several instructions whose grasps act as
each other's negatives, so no masks are needed. With the same backbone and data, \ours raises the
success rate over an additive GR-ConvNet+CLIP baseline from 45.5\% to 59.6\% on seen and from 48.2\%
to 58.9\% on unseen categories, and doubles the rate at which both grasps of an object pair succeed.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
To follow \emph{``grip the keychain by its ring''} a robot must find what the sentence refers to and
decide how a parallel gripper closes on it. Grasp-Anything++~\cite{vuong2024lgd} pairs about a million
synthetic scenes with such instructions, and detectors trained on it condition the entire network on
the text~\cite{vuong2024lgd,vo2024maskgrasp}.

Two properties of the data suggest a simpler structure. First, the opening and angle of a grasp at a
pixel follow from the object's shape; the sentence only picks the location. Second, instructions come
in groups: an image has one per annotated object, pointing to different regions. The union of their
grasps tells where the image can be grasped at all; their differences tell what each sentence adds.
\ours therefore predicts $Q=G\cdot S$. We contribute (i) a factorised detector whose geometry heads never
see the text, (ii) a sibling-contrast loss that trains selection without masks, and (iii) a paired metric
and an oracle analysis showing that selection, not geometry, limits accuracy.

\section{Related Work}
CLIPort~\cite{shridhar2021cliport} fuses CLIP~\cite{radford2021clip} into dense pick affordances;
CROG~\cite{tziafas2023crog} adds grasp maps to CLIP-based referring segmentation~\cite{wang2022cris}.
On Grasp-Anything++~\cite{vuong2024graspanything,vuong2024lgd}, LGD casts grasping as conditional
diffusion and mask-guided methods~\cite{vo2024maskgrasp,bhat2025maplegrasp} use object masks to focus
the grasp features. We multiply at the \emph{output}, keep geometry language-free and use no mask labels.
For dense CLIP features we follow MaskCLIP~\cite{zhou2022maskclip} and ClearCLIP~\cite{lan2024clearclip}.

\section{Method}
Given an image $I$ and an instruction $\ell$ we predict $g=(x,y,w,h,\theta)$.

\textbf{Graspability.} A GR-ConvNet~\cite{kumra2020grconvnet} maps $I$ to $224{\times}224$ maps of
quality logits $q$, $\cos2\theta$, $\sin2\theta$, opening $w$ and jaw size $h$; none of them sees $\ell$.
For an image with instructions $\ell_{1..K}$ and grasp sets $\mathcal{G}_{1..K}$, targets are drawn from
$\bigcup_k\mathcal{G}_k$ and
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_G=\mathrm{BCE}(q,\hat q)+\textstyle\sum_{m}\big\lVert (m-\hat m)\odot\hat q\big\rVert_{\text{sL1}},
\end{equation}
where $\hat q$ marks the centre third of each rectangle and $m$ runs over the geometry maps. Supervising
geometry only where a grasp exists matters: with GR-ConvNet's dense regression the predicted opening
collapsed to ${\sim}15$\,px (${\sim}80$\,px true) even when overfitting 70 scenes.

\textbf{Selection.} Frozen CLIP ViT-B/16 at $448^2$ gives $28{\times}28$ patch features $v_p$ from the
value path of its last block~\cite{zhou2022maskclip,lan2024clearclip}; $t$ is the text embedding.
With zero-initialised residual adapters $\phi,\psi$,
\begin{equation}
S(p\mid\ell)=\sigma\big(\tau\langle\phi(v_p),\psi(t)\rangle+b\big).
\end{equation}
For $\ell_k$, patches covered by $\mathcal{G}_k$ are positives, those covered by a sibling
$\mathcal{G}_{j\ne k}$ negatives, and the rest is ignored because $G$ already suppresses it
(class-balanced BCE).

\textbf{Inference.} $Q=\sigma(q)\cdot S$ with $S$ upsampled; the centre is $\arg\max Q$ and
$\theta,w,h$ are read from $G$. A new instruction costs one dot product.

\section{Experiments}
\textbf{Data.} We test on the official LGD splits (1{,}376 seen and 601 unseen instructions). The
official training split lists two or more objects in only 1{,}668 of its 8{,}374 images. We keep its
images that pass our filters (8{,}122) with every annotated object, and add 12{,}000 multi-object scenes
of the full release; filters exclude test scenes, object names outside the training vocabulary, and
duplicate names in an image. This gives
20{,}122 training images (118{,}292 instructions) and 500 for validation. Language mostly has to pick the
object: 80.1\% of part pairs of an object share identical grasp labels, and there are only 2{,}855
distinct sentences.

\textbf{Metrics.} \emph{Success}: IoU\,$>0.25$ and $|\Delta\theta|<30^\circ$ with a ground-truth grasp of
the instruction~\cite{vuong2024lgd}. \emph{Paired}: both grasps of an object pair in a test image succeed.
\emph{Select}: the predicted centre lies on the target object.

\textbf{Setup.} $G$ and the additive baseline (same backbone, CLIP text added at the bottleneck) train for
12 epochs (Adam, $10^{-3}$, cosine, batch 32); $S$ starts from zero-shot CLIP and trains for 4. All of it
runs on one free Colab T4 in about two hours.

\textbf{Results.} Feeding the text into the whole network adds 5.6 points over ignoring it (Table~\ref{tab:main});
multiplying the same $G$ by a selection map adds 19.7. Zero-shot CLIP already gives 55.3\%;
sibling-contrast training adds 4.3, while also treating background as negative hurts, as on validation
(object selection: zero-shot 66.3\%, all patches 64.6\%, ours 71.1\%). With perfect selection $G$
succeeds 88.9\% of the time, so the gap lies in choosing the object, not in placing the gripper.
The gain holds for unseen categories, as $S$ inherits CLIP's vocabulary, and even for images with a
single annotated object (40.7\% to 60.2\%), presumably because they contain unlabelled objects that
$G$ also finds graspable.
Training data differ from published Grasp-Anything++ results, so we only compare methods trained here.

\section{Conclusion}
Letting geometry learn from every grasp in the image and selection from the contrast between sibling
instructions is simple and effective. CLIP's $28{\times}28$ grid limits part-level selection, although
the labels rarely distinguish parts.
