\begin{abstract}
Language-driven grasp detection returns a grasp rectangle for an image and an instruction.
Existing detectors feed the instruction to the whole network, although the sentence only decides
\emph{where} to grasp, not how wide or at which angle.
We factorise grasp quality into a language-free \emph{graspability} map $G$ and an
instruction-conditioned \emph{selection} map $S$.
$G$ is a GR-ConvNet trained on the union of all grasps annotated in an image; $S$ compares frozen dense
CLIP features with the instruction and only has to choose among graspable regions.
Both are supervised by labels Grasp-Anything++ already contains: every image carries several
instructions, whose grasps serve as each other's negatives, so no masks are required.

\section{Introduction}
To follow \emph{``grip the keychain by its ring''} a robot has to decide which part of the image the
sentence means and how a parallel gripper should close on it.
Grasp-Anything++~\cite{vuong2024lgd} pairs about a million synthetic scenes with such instructions,
and methods trained on it condition the entire detector on the text~\cite{vuong2024lgd,vo2024maskgrasp}.

Two properties of the data suggest a simpler structure.
First, the opening and angle of a grasp at a pixel follow from the object's shape; the sentence only
picks the location.
Second, instructions come in groups: an image carries one for each annotated object, pointing to
different regions. The union of their grasps tells where the image can be grasped at all, and their
differences tell what each sentence adds.

\ours predicts $Q=G\cdot S$ with a language-free $G$ and a selection map $S$.
We contribute (i) a factorised detector whose geometry heads never see the text,
(ii) a sibling-contrast loss that trains selection with the other instructions of the same image
and needs no masks, and
(iii) a paired metric that a model ignoring the instruction cannot pass.

\section{Related Work}
CLIPort~\cite{shridhar2021cliport} fuses CLIP~\cite{radford2021clip} into dense pick affordances, and
CROG~\cite{tziafas2023crog} extends CLIP-based referring segmentation~\cite{wang2022cris} with grasp maps.
On Grasp-Anything++~\cite{vuong2024graspanything,vuong2024lgd}, LGD treats grasp detection as
conditional diffusion, while mask-guided methods~\cite{vo2024maskgrasp,bhat2025maplegrasp} gate grasp
features with object masks. We instead multiply at the \emph{output}, keep geometry language-free, and use
no mask labels. For dense CLIP features we follow MaskCLIP~\cite{zhou2022maskclip} (value embedding of the
last layer) and ClearCLIP~\cite{lan2024clearclip} (no residual or feed-forward path in that layer).

\section{Method}
Given an image $I$ and an instruction $\ell$ we predict $g=(x,y,w,h,\theta)$.

\textbf{Graspability.}
A GR-ConvNet~\cite{kumra2020grconvnet} maps $I$ to $224{\times}224$ maps of quality logits $q$,
$\cos2\theta$, $\sin2\theta$, opening $w$ and jaw size $h$, none of which see $\ell$.
For an image with instructions $\ell_{1..K}$ and grasp sets $\mathcal{G}_{1..K}$, targets are drawn from
$\bigcup_k\mathcal{G}_k$ and
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_G=\mathrm{BCE}(q,\hat q)+\textstyle\sum_{m}\big\lVert (m-\hat m)\odot\hat q\big\rVert_{\text{sL1}},
\end{equation}
where $\hat q$ marks the centre third of each rectangle and $m$ ranges over the four geometry maps.
Supervising geometry only where a grasp exists matters: with GR-ConvNet's dense regression the
predicted opening collapsed to ${\sim}15$\,px (${\sim}80$\,px true) even when overfitting 70 scenes.

\textbf{Selection.}
Frozen CLIP ViT-B/16 at $448^2$ gives $28{\times}28$ patch features $v_p$; $t$ is the CLIP text embedding.
With zero-initialised residual adapters $\phi,\psi$,
\begin{equation}
S(p\mid\ell)=\sigma\big(\tau\langle\phi(v_p),\psi(t)\rangle+b\big).
\end{equation}
For $\ell_k$, patches covered by $\mathcal{G}_k$ are positives, patches covered by a sibling
$\mathcal{G}_{j\ne k}$ are negatives, and the rest is ignored because $G$ already suppresses it;
we minimise a class-balanced BCE.

\textbf{Inference.}
$Q=\sigma(q)\cdot S$ ($S$ upsampled); the centre is $\arg\max Q$ and $\theta,w,h$ are read from $G$.
The backbone runs once per image; another instruction costs one dot product.

\section{Experiments}
\textbf{Data.} Test sets are the official LGD splits: 1{,}376 instructions of seen and 601 of unseen
categories. The official training split lists two or more objects in only 1{,}668 of its 8{,}374 images.
We keep every annotated object of those images and add 12{,}000 multi-object scenes of the full release,
requiring that no scene is in a test split, all object names are in the training vocabulary (no unseen
category leaks), and no two objects share a name. This yields 20{,}122 training images (17{,}572
multi-object, 118{,}292 instructions) and 500 validation images.
Two facts shape the task: 80.1\% of part pairs of the same object have identical grasp labels, and the
instructions contain only 2{,}855 distinct sentences, so language mostly has to pick the object.

\textbf{Metrics.} \emph{Success}: IoU\,$>0.25$ and $|\Delta\theta|<30^\circ$ with a ground-truth grasp of the
instruction~\cite{vuong2024lgd}. \emph{Paired}: in multi-object test images, both grasps of an object
pair must succeed; a model that ignores the text scores near zero. \emph{Select}: the predicted centre lies
on the target object.

\textbf{Setup.} $G$ and the additive baseline train for 12 epochs (Adam, $10^{-3}$, cosine, batch 32)
and $S$ for 10, on one Colab T4.

\section{Conclusion}
Separating how to grasp from what to grasp lets each part learn from the supervision it needs:
geometry from every grasp in the image, selection from the contrast between sibling instructions.
CLIP's $28{\times}28$ grid limits part-level selection, although the labels rarely distinguish parts.
