\begin{abstract}
Language-driven grasp detectors feed the instruction to the whole network, although the sentence only
decides \emph{where} to grasp, not how wide or at which angle. We factorise grasp quality into a
language-free \emph{graspability} map $G$ and an instruction-conditioned \emph{selection} map $S$.
$G$ is a GR-ConvNet trained on the union of all grasps annotated in an image; $S$ compares frozen dense
CLIP features with the instruction and only has to choose among graspable regions. Both are supervised
by labels Grasp-Anything++ already has: every image carries several instructions whose grasps act as
each other's negatives, so no masks are needed. With the same grasp backbone and training data, \ours raises
the success rate over an additive GR-ConvNet\,+\,CLIP baseline from 45.5\% to 59.6\% on seen and from 48.2\%
to 58.9\% on unseen categories, and doubles the rate at which both grasps of an object pair succeed.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
To follow \emph{``grip the keychain by its ring''} a robot must find what the sentence refers to and
decide how a parallel gripper closes on it. The public release of Grasp-Anything++~\cite{vuong2024lgd} pairs
about 0.8M synthetic scenes with 4.4M such instructions, and detectors trained on it condition the
entire network on the text~\cite{vuong2024lgd,vo2024maskgrasp}.

Two properties of the data point to a simpler design. First, the opening and angle of a grasp at a
pixel follow from the object's shape, so the sentence only has to pick the location. Second, each image
comes with one instruction per annotated object. Together, their grasps mark everything in the image
that can be grasped, and each instruction only singles out some of these regions. \ours therefore
predicts $Q=G\cdot S$. We contribute (i) a factorised detector whose geometry heads never
see the text, (ii) a sibling-contrast loss that trains selection without masks, and (iii) a paired metric
and an oracle analysis showing that selection, not geometry, limits accuracy.

\section{Related Work}
CLIPort~\cite{shridhar2021cliport} fuses CLIP~\cite{radford2021clip} into dense pick affordances;
CROG~\cite{tziafas2023crog} adds grasp maps to CLIP-based referring segmentation~\cite{wang2022cris}.
On Grasp-Anything++~\cite{vuong2024lgd}, built on Grasp-Anything~\cite{vuong2024graspanything},
LGD casts grasping as conditional diffusion and mask-guided methods~\cite{vo2024maskgrasp,bhat2025maplegrasp} use object masks to focus
the grasp features. We multiply at the \emph{output}, keep geometry language-free and use no mask labels.
For dense CLIP features we follow MaskCLIP~\cite{zhou2022maskclip} and ClearCLIP~\cite{lan2024clearclip}.

\section{Method}
Given an image $I$ and an instruction $\ell$ we predict $g=(x,y,w,h,\theta)$.

\para{Graspability.}A GR-ConvNet~\cite{kumra2020grconvnet} maps $I$ to $224{\times}224$ maps of
quality logits $q$, $\cos2\theta$, $\sin2\theta$, opening $w$ and jaw size $h$; none of them sees $\ell$.
For an image with instructions $\ell_{1..K}$ and grasp sets $\mathcal{G}_{1..K}$, targets are drawn from
$\bigcup_k\mathcal{G}_k$ and
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_G=\mathrm{BCE}(q,\hat q)+\textstyle\sum_{m}\big\lVert (m-\hat m)\odot\hat q\big\rVert_{\text{sL1}},
\end{equation}
where $\hat q$ marks the centre third of each rectangle and $m$ runs over the geometry maps.
Restricting the geometry loss to grasp pixels was necessary. With GR-ConvNet's dense regression, the
predicted opening collapsed to ${\sim}15$\,px against ${\sim}80$\,px in the labels, even when
overfitting 70 scenes.

\para{Selection.}Frozen CLIP ViT-B/16 at $448^2$ gives $28{\times}28$ patch features $v_p$ from the
value path of its last block~\cite{zhou2022maskclip,lan2024clearclip}; $t$ is the text embedding.
With zero-initialised residual adapters $\phi,\psi$,
\begin{equation}
S(p\mid\ell)=\sigma\big(\tau\langle\phi(v_p),\psi(t)\rangle+b\big).
\end{equation}
For $\ell_k$, patches covered by $\mathcal{G}_k$ are positives, those covered by a sibling
$\mathcal{G}_{j\ne k}$ negatives, and the rest is ignored because $G$ already suppresses it
(class-balanced BCE).

\para{Inference.}$Q=\sigma(q)\cdot S$ with $S$ upsampled; the centre is $\arg\max Q$ and
$\theta,w,h$ are read from $G$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:qual}). A new instruction costs one dot product per patch.

\section{Experiments}
\para{Data.}We test on the official LGD splits, keeping the 515 seen and 212 unseen objects whose
instructions are released (1{,}376 and 601 instructions). The
official training split lists two or more objects in only 1{,}668 of its 8{,}374 images. We keep its
images that pass our filters (8{,}122) with every annotated object, and add 12{,}000 multi-object scenes
of the full release; filters exclude test scenes, object names outside the training vocabulary, and
duplicate names in an image. This gives
20{,}122 training images (118{,}292 instructions) and 500 for validation. In 80.1\% of part pairs of
the same object the grasp labels are identical, and our subset has only 2{,}855 distinct sentences,
so the instruction mainly serves to pick the object.

\para{Metrics.}\emph{Success}: IoU $>0.25$ and $|\Delta\theta|<30^\circ$ with a ground-truth grasp of
the instruction~\cite{vuong2024lgd}. The next two use multi-object test images only. \emph{Paired}: both
grasps of an object pair succeed. \emph{Select}: the predicted centre falls inside the ground-truth
rectangles of the target object.

\para{Setup.}$G$ and the additive baseline (same backbone, CLIP text added at the bottleneck) train for
12 epochs (Adam, $10^{-3}$, cosine, batch 32); $S$ starts from zero-shot CLIP and trains for 4. All of it
runs on one free Colab T4 in about two hours.

\para{Results.}Adding the text to the whole network gains 5.6 points over ignoring it
(Table~\ref{tab:main}), while multiplying the same $G$ by a selection map gains 19.7. Part of this gap
comes from CLIP's image encoder, which $S$ uses and the baseline does not, so the comparison does not
isolate the factorisation. Within \ours, zero-shot CLIP already reaches 55.3\% and sibling-contrast
training adds 4.3 points. Also treating background patches as negatives lowers accuracy, as it does on
validation (object selection 66.3\% zero-shot, 64.6\% all patches, 71.1\% ours). Restricting $G$ to the
ground-truth rectangles gives 88.9\%, so most remaining errors come from picking the wrong object.
The gain carries over to unseen categories, presumably because $S$ inherits CLIP's vocabulary, and to
images with a single annotated object (40.7\% to 60.2\%), which likely contain unlabelled objects that
$G$ also finds graspable. Our training data differ from published Grasp-Anything++ results, so we only
compare methods trained here.

\section{Conclusion}
Geometry can be learned from every grasp in an image and selection from the contrast between sibling
instructions, without extra labels. The main limitation is CLIP's $28{\times}28$ grid, which is too coarse
for small parts, although the current labels rarely tell parts apart.
